A wardrobe app for people who own plenty and still stand there deciding. Named, positioned, messaged and taken to launch — the whole thing from nothing.

Lead: PMM / GTM Support: Brand Spec 6 min read
01 / Problem

A full wardrobe and nothing to wear is not a joke about shopping. It is a memory problem.

People who love clothes accumulate faster than they can recall. The jacket bought in March is invisible by June. The result is a daily decision made under time pressure, with incomplete information, about something the person genuinely cares about — which is the exact recipe for defaulting to the same four outfits and feeling vaguely disappointed about it.

The existing apps treat this as an inventory problem. Photograph everything, tag everything, maintain a database. That is a second job, and the people most likely to need the help are the least likely to finish the setup.

02 / Hypothesis

The desire to be your best self is innate and insanely human. That is what the product is actually for.

So the job is not cataloguing. Cataloguing is the tax. The job is removing the cognitive load between wanting to look like yourself and managing it before the door closes behind you.

If the app earns its keep, it does so by making the decision disappear — not by making the archive complete.

03 / Approach

Naming: Vastr

Vastr is Sanskrit for clothing, and it carries vast inside it in English. That is the wardrobe, and that is the reason you are standing in front of it. One word doing two jobs in two languages.

  • i.Debra — killed. It was reaching for a Jarvis feeling, a companion with a human name. But a human name sets the expectation of conversational AI, and this product is a wardrobe, not a butler. The promise did not match the thing.
  • ii.Whear (“what to wear”) — killed. Recall and pronunciation both fought it, and it welded the brand to a single question. No room left to expand into anything beyond outfit choice.
  • iii.Vastr — survived. Short, ownable, pronounceable on first sight, and it does not box in the roadmap.

Positioning

For people who want to dress well without the cognitive overload of deciding, Vastr catalogues what they already own — from tags, descriptions or just photos — and hands back outfits they can actually wear. Unlike Whering, Indyx, Acloset, Save Your Wardrobe and Stylebook, which ask you to finish building the database before the product does anything useful.

Messaging hierarchy

Primary: You already own the outfit. Vastr just finds it.

  • i.Ease of choice. The decision is made before you reach the wardrobe, not in front of it.
  • ii.Time you get back. The minutes are small and daily, which is exactly why they add up.
  • iii.Peace of mind. Leaving the house without the low hum of having got it slightly wrong.

Launch narrative

  • i.Tease the tension, not the product. The full-wardrobe-nothing-to-wear moment, shot as the small daily indignity it is. No app, no logo reveal, no feature.
  • ii.Name the culprit. Reframe the problem publicly: it is not that you have nothing, it is that you cannot remember what you have.
  • iii.Show the shortcut. First product moment, positioned strictly as relief rather than capability.
  • iv.Live, with proof from real wardrobes. Hand it to people whose taste the audience already trusts, and let their actual clothes carry the demonstration.

04 / Result

The one-page GTM brief

  • i.Objective. Land the category reframe — from wardrobe cataloguing to outfit recall — and convert on relief, not novelty.
  • ii.Audience. People who care how they look and own enough to lose track. Skews urban, skews fashion-literate, not necessarily high-spend.
  • iii.Positioning. The wardrobe you already have, made findable.
  • iv.Channels. Short-form video where getting dressed is already native content; creator seeding over paid social at launch; App Store copy written against the reframe, not the feature list.
  • v.Measurement. The honest leading indicator is not downloads, it is whether someone opens it on day seven. Onboarding completion, first outfit accepted, week-one return.

No projections, no invented benchmarks. Where a number would sit, the reasoning sits instead — because a made-up conversion rate proves nothing except a willingness to make things up.

05 / Learning

Both names I killed died the same way: they promised something the product could not keep. Debra promised a companion, Whear promised one question forever. A name is a contract, and you find out by writing the roadmap underneath it.
