Cut The Noise

The campaign that wins back Hungry Jack's by making the most interesting move: leaving the feed.

4 min readShrey Mehra

Spec work. Real brand, fictional brief, disclosed here.

CUT THE NOISE
Problem

Hungry Jack's is not losing to McDonald's. It's losing to the feed

The product is fine. The food is the same food, the price is competitive, the locations exist. What's gone is the reason to be there. In an era where every QSR has an app, a loyalty point, a scroll-friendly ad and a delivery window, the experience of actually being in a Hungry Jack's has been reduced to a transaction. Fast food has become frictionless food, and frictionless is forgettable.

The brand can't out-content the content. There are not enough impressions in the budget to beat the algorithm at its own game. So the brief became: what if we left the game entirely?

Hypothesis

Restraint is a campaign

The highest-signal thing a QSR brand can do in a saturated attention market is create an experience worth talking about. Not an ad worth watching. An experience worth describing to someone later.

Anti-content is content. Restraint is a campaign.
The insight

People are not tired of Hungry Jack's

They are tired of being treated like a session. An order number, a loyalty tier, a retargeting pixel. The moment a brand makes a customer feel like a person in a room rather than a unit in a funnel, the contrast is so sharp it becomes the thing they tell someone about.

The idea

Remove what everyone else is adding

Low-Noise Zone

Select stores in major cities run a one-week Low-Noise Zone. Screens dimmed. Ambient sound replaced with natural restaurant noise. Staff trained to use customer names, not numbers.

Cut onions. Cut the noise.
The 1985 Store

Two or three stores restored to the original Hungry Jack's format for one week. No app. No QR code. No loyalty tier. No digital menu board. A printed menu, a counter, and someone who asks your name and then uses it.

Hungry Jack's has an origin story it has never spent. It is the only major QSR in the country that could never use its parent brand's name, the trademark was already taken when the franchise landed in 1971, and it built a bigger local brand than the parent anyway, under a name borrowed from a pancake mix. That is a genuinely Australian piece of brand history, sitting unused.

Cut pickles. Cut the queue.
The Number Script · live capture

Customers called by number. Confusion visible. Frustration mounting. Then the mascot walks in and calls them by their name. Filmed. No script, no actors. The real reaction is the content. Released within 48 hours.

No number. Just your name.
Tagline system

A format, not a headline

The line is built to extend, which is what makes it a platform rather than a single ad. New lines can be written for placements nobody has thought of yet without breaking the system.

  • Cut onions. Cut the noise. · master line, Low-Noise Zone
  • Cut pickles. Cut the queue. · the 1985 store
  • Cut lettuce. Cut the scroll. · the three-second social films
  • Cut cheese. Cut the small print. · no app, no QR, no loyalty tier
  • Cut tomatoes. Cut the small talk. · transit and OOH

Campaign close, used once: You're more than an order.

Channels

Where the budget doesn't go

OOH and transit carry the campaign: static, minimal copy, no QR code. Social gets three-second videos: screen goes black, sound cuts, then the sizzle of a burger and ambient restaurant noise. No voiceover. Paid spend is minimal and only activates behind proven earned content.

Result

What would be measured

Spec. Aided brand recall in the target demographic pre and post campaign. Foot traffic differential between activated and non-activated stores. Earned media volume from the live-capture content. Share of organic conversation.

The hypothesis: the Low-Noise Zone generates coverage that a paid media spend of equivalent value could not buy.

Learning

The right question was never how to stand out in the feed

Anti-content is the strategy, not the tactic. The brands winning in earned media are the ones creating moments real enough that people pull out their phones voluntarily, not because they were prompted to, but because the thing they just experienced is worth sharing. That loop is not replicable with ad spend. It has to be earned through the quality of the physical moment.

A brief that asks “how do we stand out in the feed?” is the wrong brief. The right question is: what would make someone look up from it?
