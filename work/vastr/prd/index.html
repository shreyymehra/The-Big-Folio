Vastr PRD v0.1

The requirements document behind the case study. What gets built, what does not, and how we would know it worked.

9 min readShrey MehraOwner: PMM

Spec work, disclosed here, once. Vastr is fictional and nothing here shipped. This is the document as it would actually be written: scoped, prioritised, and honest about what is unknown.

Why this exists
A case study argues. A PRD commits. This is the artefact that turns the Vastr positioning into something an engineer could estimate and a founder could cut.
Status
Draft. Pre-build, pre-funding, pre-user.
Decision needed
Sign-off on the P0 scope below, which is deliberately smaller than the case study describes.
01 / Problem

The job is navigation, not acquisition

People own more clothes than any generation before them and still stand in front of a full wardrobe with nothing to wear. Every funded product in this category has read that as a cataloguing problem and built a database the user has to feed by hand.

The wardrobe is not under-stocked. It is unnavigable. That distinction is the whole product, and it is also the reason the incumbents keep losing people at week three: a database nobody maintains returns nothing, so the app that asked for the effort is the app that gets deleted.

We are not competing on catalogue size. We are competing on how little the user has to do before the product is useful.
02 / Users

Who this is for, and who it is not

Primary. 18–30, style-aware, time-poor, decision-fatigued. Owns more than they wear. Would dress better if they could find what they already have.

Secondary. The same person six months later, now using the wardrobe to decide what not to buy. This is where the affiliate model earns and where retention actually lives.

Explicitly not for, in v1: resellers, stylists managing other people's wardrobes, and anyone whose primary need is inventory for insurance or resale. Each would pull the data model toward completeness, and completeness is the thing that kills the category.

03 / Stories

User stories, P0 only

Written as the user, not as the feature. Anything that could not be phrased this way was cut before it reached this list.

  1. US-01As someone with a full wardrobe, I can add a garment by photographing it, so that building my wardrobe costs me seconds rather than an evening.
  2. US-02As someone running late, I can ask for an outfit for an occasion and get one built only from what I already own, so that I stop re-deciding the same thing every morning.
  3. US-03As someone who does not trust style apps, I can reject an outfit and say why in one tap, so that the engine learns my taste instead of a generic one.
  4. US-04As someone who has used the app for a month, I can see what I never wear, so that the wardrobe tells me something I did not already know.
  5. US-05As someone genuinely missing a piece, I can see options that fill the actual gap, so that buying is a considered decision rather than a feed.
04 / Scope

In, out, and the line between them

The case study describes three features. This document ships one and a half of them, because the outfit engine is worthless without a populated wardrobe and the shop is worthless without a working engine. Sequencing is the decision, not the feature list.

P0 · v1 ships without these and there is no product

  • Garment capture by photo, with automatic category, colour and basic attribute recognition
  • Manual correction of any recognised attribute, in one tap
  • Outfit generation from wardrobe contents against an occasion input
  • Accept / reject with a single-reason tap
  • Wardrobe browse and search

P1 · valuable, and it waits

  • The shop and affiliate layer needs an engine users already trust
  • Wear-frequency insights needs a month of real usage to say anything
  • Weather and calendar inputs adds accuracy to a system not yet proven to be wanted

Out · not in this product

  • Social feeds, sharing, following this is the engagement incentive we exist to avoid
  • Trend content and “what you should want next” the opposite of the positioning
  • Resale and listing tools pulls the data model toward completeness
05 / Requirements

What P0 actually has to do

IDRequirementWhy it is this number
R-01Capture to catalogued garment in under 10 seconds, including correction.Longer than a photo plus a glance and the user is doing data entry, which is the behaviour that loses this category.
R-02Recognition correct on category and colour without correction for the clear majority of garments.A threshold is set after the first 500 real garments, not before. Guessing a percentage here would be inventing the one number the build should produce.
R-03Any wrong attribute correctable in one tap, never a form.Correction is the recovery path for R-02. If it costs more than the error, users stop correcting and the catalogue silently rots.
R-04Outfit returned in under 3 seconds from a cold open.The competing behaviour is opening the wardrobe door. Anything slower loses to the door.
R-05Every outfit built only from owned garments. No aspirational items, ever.One suggested item the user does not own breaks the promise the whole product rests on.
R-06Rejection reason captured in one tap from a fixed, short list.Free text will not be written at 7am. A short list is the only feedback that will actually arrive.
R-07Wardrobe usable at 15 garments, not 100.Time-to-value decides week-one retention. A product that needs a full wardrobe before it works is a product nobody finishes setting up.

R-02 has no number in it on purpose. The threshold is an output of the first build, and a target invented before the data exists is the kind of number this portfolio does not print.

06 / Success

How we would know, and what would kill it

Three of these targets are carried from the GTM brief in the case study. The fourth is the only one that tests the hypothesis rather than the launch.

MetricTargetWhat it tells us
Outfit acceptance rate65%+ by day 30The one that matters. Below this the AI is guessing at taste rather than reflecting it, and the product is a worse version of the incumbents.
Downloads, first 30 days10,000Whether the positioning travels. A launch number, not a product one.
Pre-launch waitlist5,000Whether the problem is recognised before the product is seen.
Affiliate click-through8%+ within 60 days of shop launchWhether a purchase recommendation made from a real gap converts better than a feed. This is the business model's only test.
Time to 15th garmentUnder one sessionR-07 in the field. If setup spans sessions, most users never return to finish it.

The kill criterion. If acceptance rate sits below 50% at day 30 with a populated wardrobe, the hypothesis is wrong and no amount of UI fixes it. The product would be recommending, not reflecting, and the category already has five of those.

07 / Risks

What breaks this, in order of likelihood

Recognition quality is the product
Every error lands in the catalogue and the engine inherits it. Accuracy stops being the user's chore and becomes our risk. Mitigation: one-tap correction (R-03), and recognition confidence surfaced rather than hidden, so a low-confidence guess asks instead of asserting.
The engine has no taste signal on day one
A new user has zero rejections, so the first outfits are the weakest the product will ever produce, at the exact moment it is being judged. Mitigation: onboarding asks for three owned outfits the user already likes, which is a taste sample disguised as setup.
The affiliate model earns too slowly to fund the build
Revenue only arrives once the user has genuinely been served, which is the point, and also a harder story than a usage graph. Mitigation: none at the product level. This is a funding decision, and it is the trade-off the positioning was chosen with eyes open.
An incumbent copies the capture flow
It is the most visible part and the least defensible. Mitigation: the defensibility is the incentive model, not the feature. A competitor earning on engagement cannot copy a product that earns on restraint without breaking their own revenue.
08 / Release

Three phases, each with a reason to stop

  1. Phase 1 · ClosedP0 scope, invite only, target 200 wardrobes. The only question being asked: does recognition work well enough that people keep adding garments after the first session? Stop here if it does not.
  2. Phase 2 · WaitlistSame scope, opened to the waitlist. The question becomes acceptance rate against a real spread of taste. The 65% target is measured here, before any spend.
  3. Phase 3 · Launch and shopPublic, with the affiliate layer switched on. The shop ships last because a recommendation is only trusted from a product that has already proved it understands the wardrobe.
09 / Open

What this document does not answer

Listed because a PRD that pretends to be complete is the least useful kind.

  • Whether recognition should run on-device or server-side. It is a cost, latency and privacy trade-off, and R-04 is the constraint it has to satisfy.
  • What the engine does with a wardrobe of 300 garments. Every assumption here is written for 15 to 60.
  • Whether “occasion” is the right input. It is the one the case study assumes; mood and weather are the alternatives and no user has been asked.
